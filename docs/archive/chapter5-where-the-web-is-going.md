## 5.1 Introduction

The experimental chapters of this work characterise what happens inside a
single origin when traffic composition changes. That characterisation is
controlled and reproducible, but it is also narrow: it describes one
corpus, one cache topology, one bottleneck, and two synthetic access
patterns standing in for populations whose real behaviour was not, at the
time the testbed was designed, well documented.

This chapter looks outward. It asks what is actually arriving at web
origins in 2025–2026, how the mechanisms that govern that arrival are
changing, and what can — and cannot — be said about where the trajectory
leads. The motivation is not forecasting for its own sake. It is that the
design choices examined in the following chapter only matter if the
population they address is real, heterogeneous, and durable. If automated
traffic were a transient phenomenon, or a single homogeneous class,
per-class resource reasoning would be unnecessary.

Three things make the question timely. First, the share of traffic
generated by automated clients crossed the halfway mark during 2026 on
the network of the largest operator publishing such figures. Second, that
automated traffic is not one thing: training crawlers, search crawlers,
retrieval agents acting on behalf of a waiting person, SEO tooling, and
undeclared automation exhibit measurably different access patterns,
connection behaviour, and error profiles. Third, the mechanisms by which
sites express and enforce access policy are being rewritten in real time,
in standards bodies and in vendor defaults, on a timescale of months.

The chapter is organised around a strict separation that is maintained
throughout:

- **Observed evidence** — measurements, with their source, denominator,
  and observation window stated.
- **Industry response** — mechanisms proposed, standardised, or shipped,
  with their actual maturity and adoption where measurable.
- **Forecasts** — predictions made by identifiable parties, attributed,
  dated, and labelled as such.
- **Our interpretation** — inferences we draw, marked as inference.
- **Open questions** — what the available evidence does not settle.

No forecast is presented as an observed fact. Where our own honeypot data
is used, its limitations are stated alongside it.

## 5.2 The Cloudflare Network Is Already Predominantly Automated

### 5.2.1 The measurement

On 3 June 2026, Cloudflare's chief executive Matthew Prince reported that
automated requests had, for the first time, exceeded human requests on
Cloudflare's network. The figure circulated in the range **57.3%–57.5%
automated against 42.5%–42.7% human**, with minor discrepancies across
the outlets that reported it (Search Engine Land reported 57.3%/42.7%;
NBC News reported 57.4%/42.6%).

**This figure is reported here as press coverage of a statement made on
social media by a company executive.** No Cloudflare publication carrying
the figure was located during this work, and it should be cited as such
rather than as a Radar measurement.

Four qualifications are essential and are frequently omitted when the
figure is cited.

**The denominator is HTML page requests**, not all HTTP requests, not
bytes transferred, and not sessions. Cloudflare's own broader figure for
automated traffic as a share of *all* requests on its network sits
considerably lower — the April 2026 engineering blog cited approximately
32%. These are two different measurements of two different quantities and
should never be presented as alternative estimates of the same thing.

**The population is Cloudflare's network**, which the company describes
as covering roughly a fifth of the web. It is not a random sample:
Cloudflare's customer base is skewed by size, sector, and geography, and
sites that have adopted a CDN are not representative of sites that have
not.

**The classification is proprietary and acknowledged as imperfect.**
Prince himself characterised the classification as imprecise when
announcing the figure. Cloudflare sells bot management; its measurements
of bot prevalence are made with a commercial instrument by a commercially
interested party. This does not make them wrong, but it places them in
the category of vendor measurement rather than independent observation.

**The crossing point is a threshold, not a trend line.** A ratio of
57:43 between two quantities that both fluctuate is compatible with a
range of underlying dynamics.

### 5.2.2 Comparison with other reported figures

Two other widely cited sources report superficially similar numbers on
incompatible bases.

Imperva's *Bad Bot Report* for 2026 reports that automated traffic
constituted approximately **53% of all web traffic during 2025**, using
Imperva's own network and its own classification. The denominator is all
traffic, not HTML requests, and the observation year is different.

HUMAN Security's *2026 State of AI Traffic & Cyberthreat Benchmark
Report* (announced 26 March 2026) reports growth rates rather than
shares: **agentic traffic up 7,851% year on year**, AI scrapers up 597%,
and AI training crawlers constituting **67.5% of AI-driven traffic** —
where "AI-driven traffic" is HUMAN's own category, not a share of total
web traffic.

**These three figures cannot be reconciled into a single estimate.** They
use different networks, different classifiers, different denominators,
and different observation windows. What they agree on is direction: the
automated share is large and growing. That agreement, across three
commercially independent measurement platforms, is the defensible claim.

### 5.2.3 What this does and does not establish

The data establish that **traffic on the Cloudflare network is
predominantly automated** in at least one meaningful sense, and that two
other commercial measurement platforms observe the same direction on
their own networks. They do **not** establish a figure for the web as a
whole, and they do **not** establish that the web is "agentic". Bot
traffic and AI agent traffic are not the same category, and conflating
them is the single most common error in current discourse on this topic.
As Section 5.3 shows, the agentic share — traffic generated by a system
acting on behalf of a person waiting in real time — remains a small
fraction of automated traffic by every published measurement, including
our own.

## 5.3 There Is No Single "AI Traffic"

### 5.3.1 Composition by declared purpose

Cloudflare classifies AI bot traffic into Search, Agent (User Action),
and Training categories, and exposes the breakdown through the Radar API.
The weekly series retrieved for this work on **17 September 2026**,
covering 25 May to 14 September 2026 in a single query, shows the
following composition of AI bot requests at selected points:

| Week beginning | Training | Mixed Purpose | Search | **User Action** | Undeclared |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Jun 2026 | 51.6% | 39.8% | 5.2% | **2.70%** | 0.6% |
| 13 Jul 2026 | 51.7% | 40.3% | 5.0% | **2.39%** | 0.7% |
| 3 Aug 2026 | 49.6% | 35.9% | 7.7% | **5.01%** | 1.7% |
| 10 Aug 2026 | 49.3% | 32.6% | 9.3% | **6.66%** | 2.1% |
| 31 Aug 2026 | 45.6% | 42.2% | 8.6% | **2.02%** | 1.6% |
| 14 Sep 2026 | 45.7% | 40.1% | 9.8% | **2.59%** | 1.7% |

*Denominator: AI bot requests on the Cloudflare network in that week.
Source: Cloudflare Radar API, `timeseries_groups/CRAWL_PURPOSE`, weekly
aggregation, queried 17 September 2026. The most recent week of any
retrieval is provisional and changes as it completes.*

Two features of this series matter for how such figures should be used.

**The Search share rose materially over the window**, from approximately
5% in early June to approximately 10% in mid-September, while the
Training share declined from approximately 52% to 46%. This is a change
in the mix of AI bot traffic, not in its total volume, which this
normalisation does not show.

**The User Action share is both small and unstable**, moving between
2.0% and 6.7% within the same seventeen weeks. That instability is not
noise: Section 5.7.3 shows that it moves inversely to the share of 200
responses the class receives, and offers an interpretation of why.
Any single-window figure for the agentic share should therefore be
read as a point on a volatile series rather than as a level.

Cloudflare's own year-on-year comparison (User Action +130%, Search +91%,
Training +8% between August 2025 and August 2026) describes a different
timescale and is not in contradiction with the above, but citing it
alone conveys a false impression of steadiness. Cloudflare has also
relabelled categories during this period, which its own documentation
acknowledges; series spanning that relabelling should be treated as
discontinuous.

### 5.3.2 Composition observed on a single unfiltered site

For comparison, the composition observed on the measurement site operated
for this work (`theslowshelf.org`, a static archive of public-domain
literature whose `robots.txt` explicitly permits all AI crawlers and
which applies no rate limiting) over **443,232 requests across 36 days
(12 August – 16 September 2026)** was:

| Class | Share of requests |
|---|---|
| Unclassified (browser-like user agents) | 34.4% |
| SEO crawlers | 29.8% |
| AI training crawlers | 18.9% |
| Search crawlers | 8.2% |
| AI search/retrieval | 6.0% |
| **AI agent (user action)** | **1.7%** |
| Vulnerability scanners | 0.8% |
| Monitoring/tooling | 0.2% |

*Denominator: all HTTP requests to one site. Single-site observation;
not representative of the web, and not directly comparable to the
Cloudflare figures above, which are shares of AI bot requests on a
different population.*

Two observations follow. First, the agentic share (1.7%) is of the same
order as Cloudflare's weekly figures (2.0%–6.7%), which is weak
corroboration that the agentic class is genuinely small rather than an
artefact of one vendor's classifier. Second, **SEO crawlers generated
more traffic than AI training crawlers** on this site — a single SEO
operator (SemrushBot, 61,576 requests) exceeded every AI operator
individually. Public discussion of automated load focuses overwhelmingly
on AI crawlers; on this particular site, over this period, they were not
the dominant exhaustive load.

### 5.3.3 Behavioural differentiation

The categories differ not only in volume but in measurable behaviour. The
following table summarises what was observed on the measurement site,
with sources and limitations stated per row.

| Class | Purpose | Observed behaviour | Evidence | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Training crawlers** | Bulk corpus acquisition | Near-complete site coverage, Gini ≈ 0.07–0.30, ~1.0–1.5 requests per URL, arrive in concentrated bursts (peak-to-mean up to 15.9×) | Honeypot logs, 36 days | One site; coverage figures inflated by request paths that do not exist |
| **Search crawlers** | Index construction | Partial coverage (Googlebot 18.6% of pages), Gini 0.54 — strongly selective | Honeypot logs | Low coverage may reflect crawl budget on a new domain rather than policy |
| **Agentic / user action** | Answer a waiting person's query | Very low volume, short sessions, high 404 rate on declared traffic | Honeypot logs; Cloudflare Radar | See §5.6.4: a substantial share of traffic *declaring* agentic identity on this site did not originate from the operators' known infrastructure |
| **SEO / automated discovery** | Backlink and ranking analysis | Complete coverage, extremely uniform (Gini 0.001–0.037), highly regular arrival | Honeypot logs | — |
| **Browser-like automation** | Heterogeneous | 0.0% referer across 152,547 requests; most-requested paths `/.env`, `/.git/config`, `/wp-admin/install.php` | Honeypot logs | This is predominantly vulnerability scanning, not unclassified browsing |
| **Traffic declaring AI identity from unattributed sources** | Unknown | Multiple distinct declared AI operator identities observed originating from overlapping source infrastructure | Honeypot logs | See §5.6.4: source infrastructure alone does not establish authenticity or its absence |

Two behavioural measurements deserve particular note because they are
not, to our knowledge, routinely reported and they distinguish operators
more sharply than declared identity does.

**Connection reuse** varied by more than an order of magnitude across
operators: GPTBot averaged 20.4 requests per TCP connection, Googlebot
9.4, GoogleOther 5.2, while SemrushBot, AhrefsBot, DotBot,
Meta-ExternalAgent, and Amazonbot all averaged exactly 1.0. *Caveat:
nginx's `$connection` is a per-worker serial, so the (IP, connection) key
undercounts distinct connections and overcounts reuse; and
`keepalive_requests` capped observations at 1,000 for three operators
until the limit was raised on 13 September 2026, introducing a
discontinuity in this series.*

**Visit ordering**, measured as the correlation between temporal order
and alphabetical URL order, separated operators that follow the sitemap
(MJ12bot 0.992, DotBot 0.942) from one that traverses it in reverse
(AhrefsBot 0.128) and from the majority that use an internal ordering
(≈0.50). Unlike a user-agent string, this signal cannot be forged without
changing behaviour.

### 5.3.5 Working-set size within a cache-relevant window

One behavioural quantity deserves separate treatment, because it is the
parameter the experimental chapters turn out to depend on most: **how many
distinct objects a single client touches within a window short enough to
matter to a cache.**

Counting distinct URLs per client over a whole observation period answers
the wrong question. An object survives in an LRU-like cache only if it is
re-requested within the cache's characteristic time — on the testbed of
the following chapters, approximately 143 seconds. Measured over a sliding
window of that length across 41 days, the agentic class (31 clients with at
least five requests) gives:

| Statistic | Distinct URLs in a 143 s window |
|---|---|
| Median | 4 |
| Mean | 240 |
| 90th percentile | **670** |
| Maximum | 678 |

The distribution is strongly bimodal: most agentic clients touch a handful
of pages, a minority several hundred. Widening the window to 600 s or
3600 s barely moves these figures for this class. For training crawlers it
does: the 3600 s median is 129 against 12 at 143 s — that class spreads
its accesses thinly over time, which is precisely why it caches badly.

*Limitations:* 31 clients, one site, 41 days. The 90th percentile of so
small a sample is fragile, and nothing here licenses a statement about
agentic traffic on the web at large.

### 5.3.4 Interpretation

*The following is our inference, not an observation.* The heterogeneity
documented above suggests that a policy category labelled "AI traffic"
aggregates populations whose infrastructural footprints differ along at
least four independent axes: locality of access, volume, connection
behaviour, and urgency. Whether these differences translate into
materially different resource costs is the empirical question the
experimental chapters address; this chapter establishes only that the
differences in *behaviour* are measurable and large.

## 5.4 From Crawl-to-Refer to an Intermediated Web

### 5.4.1 What the ratio measures

The crawl-to-refer ratio, as published by Cloudflare Radar, is the number
of HTML page requests a platform's crawler makes to sites on Cloudflare's
network, divided by the number of HTML page referrals that platform sends
back to those sites. It is a measure of extraction relative to
observable return traffic, computed over a stated window.

### 5.4.2 Reported values

**The per-operator values below are drawn from third-party readings of
the Radar interface rather than from a Cloudflare publication, and the
observation window materially affects them.** They are reported here to
establish order of magnitude and should be re-verified against the Radar
API before submission (see §5.12).

For a 28-day window ending 21 July 2026, values so reported include
approximately 3,389:1 for Mistral, 2,237:1 for Anthropic's ClaudeBot, and
217:1 for OpenAI's GPTBot, against approximately 2.5:1 for DuckDuckGo.
Cloudflare's own June 2025 publication reported ClaudeBot at roughly
70,900:1 at that time, indicating that the ratio for individual operators
has changed by more than an order of magnitude within a year.

### 5.4.3 What can be concluded

The defensible conclusion is narrow: **the volume of content extracted by
some AI platforms is orders of magnitude larger than the referral traffic
those platforms return to the source, as measured at the CDN layer.**

It does **not** follow that agents send no traffic to sites. The ratios
vary by three orders of magnitude across operators, have moved by an
order of magnitude within a year for at least one operator, and are
measured only for traffic that both crawls and refers through the same
network. Nor does it follow that referral traffic is the correct measure
of value delivered to a publisher, which is a question about business
models rather than about infrastructure.

What the ratio does establish, for the purposes of this work, is that
**fetch volume and downstream benefit to the site are decoupled**. A
request that arrives at an origin imposes a cost whether or not it
eventually produces a visitor. This decoupling is what makes the cost of
serving a request an interesting quantity independent of its commercial
value.

### 5.4.4 Corroborating evidence on referral decline

Independent measurements of the referral side are more rigorous than the
vendor-reported ratios.

The Pew Research Center, analysing **68,879 real searches performed by
900 US adults in March 2025**, found that users clicked a traditional
search result in 8% of visits where an AI Overview was present, against
15% where it was not, and that only 1% of AI Overview presentations
resulted in a click on a cited source.

A randomised study published on SSRN (3 April 2026, revised 17 June 2026)
using **1,065 Chrome users** reported organic outbound clicks down 39.8%
and zero-click sessions up 34.5% in the presence of an AI Overview. This
is, to our knowledge, the first causal rather than correlational estimate
of the effect.

Industry measurements point the same way with less methodological
control: Digital Content Next reported a median 10% decline in Google
referrals across 19 member publishers (May–June 2025), and Chartbeat
reported a 33% decline in Google referrals across more than 2,500 news
sites during 2025.

Google has publicly contested this framing, stating in May 2025 that the
web "is thriving", and in July 2025 disputed the Pew methodology on the
grounds that the observation period overlapped algorithm tests unrelated
to AI Overviews.

## 5.5 Agents Can Answer Without Visiting the Site

### 5.5.1 Existing literature

The Tow Center for Digital Journalism at Columbia University published an
evaluation in March 2025 (Jaźwińska and Chandrasekar) of eight AI search
products across **1,600 queries**. It reported incorrect answers in more
than 60% of cases overall, ranging from 37% for Perplexity to 94% for
Grok-3. Two failure modes documented there bear directly on
infrastructure: AI search products frequently cited **syndicated or
copied versions of content rather than the original publisher**, and
frequently produced **fabricated or broken URLs**.

The implication, which the Tow Center study does not itself frame in
infrastructural terms, is that the relationship between "an AI system
answering a question about a site" and "that site receiving a request" is
not one-to-one.

### 5.5.2 An exploratory experiment

To examine this relationship directly, we conducted a small elicitation
test on 16 September 2026. Twelve questions whose answers could be
obtained only by reading the measurement site were put to three
commercial assistants with browsing enabled (ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity),
four questions each, spaced five minutes apart, with timestamps recorded.
The site's request log was then examined for the corresponding windows.

**Of twelve questions, four produced an observable fetch of the site.**

| Assistant | Questions | Fetches observed | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| ChatGPT | 4 | 2 | Answered two questions by substituting Project Gutenberg and other indexed sources |
| Claude | 4 | 2 | Two further questions failed at the discovery stage: the assistant reported being unable to locate the site through search |
| Perplexity | 4 | 0 | Answered from archive.org, Project Gutenberg and secondary sources in all four cases |

Three observations, offered as observations about this specific test:

**Source substitution occurred repeatedly.** In eight of twelve cases the
assistant produced an answer without requesting the page. In one instance
the assistant stated that it had verified the content on the measurement
site while citing Project Gutenberg. From the site operator's
perspective, the site is referenced without being visited.

**One assistant performed genuine navigation.** The Claude-User requests
followed a link path — `robots.txt`, then the home page, then
`authors.html`, then an author page, then a book index (incurring one 301
redirect), then the chapter — using eight TCP connections for eight
requests. It was the only operator observed in the entire 36-day dataset
to request `robots.txt` before requesting content.

**Discoverability, not reachability, was the binding constraint in two
cases.** The site was reachable and unblocked, but was not present in the
search index the assistant consulted, and the assistant declined to
construct a URL. This suggests that an agent's ability to reach a site
may depend on the site's presence in a search index rather than on the
site's own access policy — an observation that, if it generalises, has
implications for how sites should expose themselves to agents.

### 5.5.3 Limitations

**This is an exploratory test with n = 12 and must not be generalised.**
Twelve questions, one site, one day, three assistants, one account, one
set of model versions. Assistant behaviour depends on model version,
interface mode, account configuration, and the phrasing of the prompt,
none of which were varied systematically. The finding is a demonstration
that citation without retrieval occurs and can be measured by an operator
with instrumented logs — not a quantification of how often it occurs.

The concept the test illustrates — **citation without retrieval** — is
consistent with the Tow Center findings and, to our knowledge, has not
been quantified from the server side in published work. Establishing a
rate would require a substantially larger and more systematic protocol.

## 5.6 From robots.txt to Agent Authentication

The mechanisms by which a site expresses and enforces access policy are
being rewritten. This section states, for each mechanism, what it
proposes, its standardisation status as of September 2026, and its
measured adoption where such measurement exists. Section 5.6.7
summarises.

### 5.6.1 robots.txt (RFC 9309)

The existing mechanism: advisory, not enforced, and predating any
distinction between indexing and model training. Compliance is partial
and has been measured. Kim et al. (IMC 2025) observed 130 declared bots
over 40 days across 36 sites (approximately 3.9 million requests) and
reported a mean compliance rate of 0.397, with SEO crawlers most
compliant (0.704) and AI assistants at 0.665. **That study measured
compliance with `robots.txt` directives and crawl-delay; it did not
measure compliance with HTTP-level signals such as 503 or 429
responses**, a gap relevant to any deferral-based policy.

### 5.6.2 llms.txt

A proposed convention for exposing a curated, machine-readable summary of
a site's content at a well-known path. **Standardisation: none** — it is
a community convention, and Google stated publicly in July 2025 that it
does not support it.

Publication and consumption diverge sharply. Originality.AI's tracking
reports growth from 4,088 files (June 2025) to 36,120 (May 2026). But
Ahrefs, examining server logs across approximately 137,000 domains,
reported that **97% of llms.txt files received no requests at all during
May 2026**, that AI retrieval bots accounted for 1.1% of requests to such
files (GPTBot 4.51%, ClaudeBot 0.80%), and that the largest single
category of requester was SEO audit tooling at 21.7%. Ahrefs further
observed that AI bots do not probe for the file on domains where it does
not exist. SE Ranking, examining approximately 300,000 domains, found no
correlation between the presence of llms.txt and citation frequency by AI
systems.

**llms.txt should therefore be described as a published convention with
substantial authoring adoption and near-zero measured consumption.**

### 5.6.3 IETF AI Preferences (aipref)

A chartered IETF working group producing a vocabulary for expressing
preferences about AI use of content and a mechanism for attaching them.
The active drafts are `draft-ietf-aipref-vocab`, at revision **-08 of
13 September 2026**, and `draft-ietf-aipref-attach`, at revision **-05 of
18 August 2026** (verified against the IETF Datatracker on 22 September
2026). The mechanism defines a `Content-Usage`
HTTP header (for example `Content-Usage: train-ai=n`) built on RFC 9651
structured fields, and **explicitly updates RFC 9309**. Editors include
Gary Illyes (Google), Martin Thomson (Mozilla) and Paul Keller.

**Status: work in progress. No RFC published, no adoption measurement
available.**

### 5.6.4 Web Bot Auth

A mechanism for cryptographically authenticating automated clients, built
on HTTP Message Signatures (RFC 9421) with Ed25519 keys and a
`Signature-Agent` header.

**Status as of September 2026: IETF standardisation in progress, not
complete.** The `webbotauth` working group was chartered on 23 October
2025. The first working-group document,
`draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol-00`, was published on
1 September 2026, superseding earlier individual drafts
(`draft-meunier-web-bot-auth-architecture`, which reached revision -05).
The work is on the Standards Track. **It must not be described as a
finished standard.** Deployment nonetheless precedes standardisation:
Cloudflare, AWS WAF, Akamai, HUMAN and Vercel verify signatures in
production, and Anthropic, OpenAI, Perplexity and Common Crawl sign
requests.

On the measurement site, over 36 days, **exactly one operator of
twenty-nine sent Web Bot Auth signature headers**: AhrefsBot, on all
19,744 of its requests. A later retrieval covering 41 days (12 August –
21 September 2026, 520,038 classified requests) gives **19,930 signed
requests, 3.83% of the total** — and, notably, **none from clients
declaring an agentic user agent**. In this sample, signing and declaring
oneself an agent were disjoint behaviours. That same operator accounted for 500 of the 561
conditional requests observed once the relevant log fields were added on
13 September 2026, and for 531 of the 533 `304 Not Modified` responses
issued. It also averaged 1.0 requests per TCP connection — the least
efficient transport behaviour observed among high-volume operators.

*Our interpretation, stated as interpretation:* in this sample,
cryptographic identity co-occurred with strong HTTP protocol conformance
and with poor transport efficiency. This is an association in a sample of
one signing operator, not a causal relationship, and not a general
property of signed traffic. What it illustrates is that "cost" is not a
single quantity: an admission policy that admits signed operators is
making a choice about *which* cost it optimises, and that choice is not
neutral.

**On source infrastructure and authenticity.** During this work,
overlapping source addresses were observed presenting several different
declared AI operator identities. A subsequent controlled test, in which
genuine agent requests were elicited by querying commercial assistants,
established that **authentic Claude-User requests originate from Google
Cloud infrastructure** — the same category of infrastructure from which
the anomalous traffic originated. **Source infrastructure therefore does
not, by itself, establish either authenticity or its absence**, and any
earlier inference of that kind is withdrawn. The observation that
survives is narrower: *the same source infrastructure was observed
presenting multiple distinct declared AI operator identities*, which is
consistent with impersonation but does not demonstrate it. Building a
reference set of verified operator addresses would require a
substantially larger elicitation campaign than the one conducted here.

### 5.6.5 Content representation for agents

Cloudflare introduced "Markdown for Agents" on 12 February 2026:
on-the-fly conversion of HTML to Markdown via content negotiation
(`Accept: text/markdown`), with a reported token reduction of up to 80%
relative to the HTML source, and existing client support (Claude Code,
OpenCode). This is a delivery-layer optimisation: it addresses the cost of
consuming content, not the cost of discovering or serving it, and no
independent measurement of its effect on origin load has been published.

### 5.6.6 Tool-mediated access (MCP)

The Model Context Protocol has become a common channel for agents to
access external systems, with a substantial ecosystem of servers for web
retrieval; some approaches bypass HTML retrieval entirely by calling a
site's internal APIs. **MCP is not currently a replacement for HTTP
fetching at web scale**: it requires the site to expose a server or the
agent to have discovered an API, and no published measurement quantifies
what fraction of agent access occurs through such channels. Its
infrastructural significance, if adoption grows, is that it would move
load from HTML delivery to application endpoints — a shift in where cost
is incurred rather than a reduction in cost.

### 5.6.7 Summary

| Mechanism | Standardisation | Measured adoption | Principal limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| robots.txt | RFC 9309 | Universal publication; compliance ≈ 0.40 mean (Kim et al., IMC 2025) | Advisory; predates the training/indexing distinction |
| llms.txt | None (convention) | 36,120 files (May 2026); 97% received zero requests | Published but not consumed |
| aipref | IETF WG drafts, no RFC | Not measured | In progress |
| Web Bot Auth | IETF WG draft (Sept 2026), Standards Track | Deployed by major verifiers; 1 of 29 operators signing on our site | Not yet a standard; authenticates identity, not behaviour |
| Markdown for Agents | Vendor feature | Not independently measured | Delivery-layer only |
| MCP | Open protocol, rapid ecosystem growth | Not measured as a share of agent access | Requires per-site provision |

## 5.7 The Industry Response: Control, Authentication, Monetisation

### 5.7.1 The direction

Across the mechanisms above, a consistent direction is visible in the
infrastructure layer: **identify the traffic, classify it by declared
purpose, apply differentiated policy, and where possible attach a price.**
This is a shift from the advisory model of `robots.txt` toward enforced,
network-level policy with commercial terms.

Cloudflare is treated here as a case study rather than as evidence of
industry-wide convergence. It is the most documented actor because it
publishes; other CDNs and infrastructure providers have made related
moves with less public documentation, and the extent of convergence
cannot be established from public sources.

### 5.7.2 The 15 September 2026 default change

Announced on 1 July 2026 and **stated to take effect on 15 September
2026**, Cloudflare described a change to its default handling of AI crawler
traffic. The wording matters. The only primary source located is the
prospective announcement of 1 July 2026; a systematic search on 22 September
2026 found **no Cloudflare post or changelog dated on or near 15 September
confirming activation**. Several third-party accounts describe the change as
a network-wide default block, and the primary text does not support that
reading. The change, as documented in the announcement:

- On pages that serve advertising, crawlers classified as **Training** and
  **Agent** are blocked by default; crawlers classified as **Search**
  remain permitted.
- The changelog text specifies **new domains onboarding to Cloudflare**.
  The blog post is read by some sources as extending to new sites added by
  existing customers and to existing free-tier customers; this wider scope
  **could not be confirmed against a primary source and should be verified
  or dropped before submission**. Paying customers with existing
  configurations retain them.
- Multi-purpose crawlers (Googlebot, Bingbot, Applebot) are handled under
  the most restrictive applicable rule.
- Enforcement is at the network layer, independent of `robots.txt`.

Cloudflare simultaneously introduced a usage-based payment mechanism
(an evolution of its earlier pay-per-crawl offering) with initial
partners.

**No post-activation measurement had been published at the time of
writing.** Our own Radar retrieval of 17 September 2026 covers only two
days following the change, which is insufficient to detect an effect.

### 5.7.3 A caution on reading the response-code series

Cloudflare Radar's weekly series for response codes received by the
User Action class, retrieved in a single query spanning 25 May to
14 September 2026 with identical parameters throughout, shows a large
change during July 2026.

| Week beginning | 200 | 403 | 404 | 429 | User Action share of AI bot requests |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Jun 2026 | 56.0% | 19.2% | 3.3% | 1.2% | 2.70% |
| 6 Jul 2026 | 55.0% | 24.1% | 2.8% | 1.1% | 2.48% |
| 13 Jul 2026 | 51.3% | 23.9% | 5.1% | 2.0% | 2.39% |
| 20 Jul 2026 | 37.1% | 26.9% | 13.6% | 4.3% | 2.80% |
| 3 Aug 2026 | 23.8% | 34.1% | 19.6% | 7.4% | 5.01% |
| 10 Aug 2026 | 21.4% | 36.7% | 20.1% | 7.5% | 6.66% |
| 31 Aug 2026 | 52.8% | 21.2% | 8.1% | 3.3% | 2.02% |
| 14 Sep 2026 | 30.0% | 31.5% | 16.6% | 4.9% | 2.59% |

*Denominator for the response-code columns: responses to requests
classified as User Action on the Cloudflare network in that week. The
figures are shares of a response-code distribution, not a rate of
successful task completion; redirects (301 and 302 combined) account for
a further 5.2% in June rising to 10.3% in September and are neither
successes nor refusals. Denominator for the final column: all AI bot
requests. Source: Cloudflare Radar API, queried 17 September 2026. The
most recent week of any retrieval is provisional and changes as it
completes: the week of 14 September read 31.5% for 200 responses when
retrieved on 16 September and 30.0% when retrieved on 17 September.*

Three observations constrain how this series may be read.

**The change is concentrated in the week of 20 July 2026** and is
substantial: the share of 200 responses falls from 51.3% to 23.8% in
three weeks. This is the largest movement in the seventeen-week window.

**The week beginning 31 August is not a recovery.** In that week the 200
share rises for all three classes simultaneously — Training from 63.9%
to 71.6%, Search from 43.5% to 60.4%, User Action from 21.6% to 52.8% —
before returning to prior levels. A movement common to all three classes
is not evidence about the treatment of any one of them, and this week
should be excluded from any characterisation of the agentic class.

**The share of the class moves inversely to its share of 200
responses.** Between early July and early August the User Action share of
AI bot requests approximately doubled, from 2.4% to 6.7%, while its share
of 200 responses fell from 51% to 21%; in the week of 31 August the class
share fell to 2.02%, its lowest in the window, while the 200 share rose
to 52.8%. *Our interpretation, offered as interpretation and not as a
demonstrated fact:* this pattern is more consistent with a change in the
composition of the class than with a change in how its members are
treated. A population entering the classification that is both larger and
more frequently refused would produce this signature. The Radar data
alone cannot distinguish the two explanations, and both may be operating.

The composition of the decline is consistent with that reading. Between
13 July and 14 September, refusals (403 and 429 combined) rose from 25.8%
to 36.4%, while responses indicating that the requested resource does not
exist (404) rose from 5.1% to 16.6%. **Roughly half the decline in 200
responses is attributable to requests for URLs that do not exist**, which
is a property of the requester rather than a policy of the site.
Section 5.6.4 documents, on a single instrumented site, traffic declaring
agentic identity from unattributed sources and exhibiting high 404 rates;
whether the global signal has the same origin cannot be determined from
Radar data.

The defensible statements are therefore narrower than the raw series
suggests. For the week of 14 September 2026, on the Cloudflare network:
**the User Action class received a refusal (403 or 429) in 36.4% of
responses, against 23.6% for Search and 20.8% for Training**; and it
received a 200 response in 30.0% of cases, against 49.5% and 61.7%
respectively. The ordering — the class in which a person is waiting is
refused most often, the Training class least often — holds in every week
of the seventeen examined. **What cannot be established from these data
is whether the increase in refusals over 2026 reflects a change in
operator policy, a change in the composition of the traffic being
classified, or both.**

### 5.7.4 Licensing and payment mechanisms

Really Simple Licensing (RSL), launched 10 September 2025 with
specification 1.0 finalised in December 2025, provides a machine-readable
licensing vocabulary expressed through `robots.txt`, HTTP, and RSS, and
supports pay-per-crawl and pay-per-inference terms. It is administered by
the RSL Collective and has attracted more than fifty publisher partners.

**Publisher-side adoption is substantial; AI-company-side adoption is not
demonstrated by available sources.** A licensing mechanism that the
licensee does not honour is a declaration, not a transaction.

## 5.8 What Can Actually Be Predicted

### 5.8.1 Observed data

The following are measurements, with the qualifications already stated:

- Automated requests exceeded human requests on the Cloudflare network
  during 2026, measured as a share of HTML page requests and reported
  through press coverage of an executive statement.
- The agentic share of AI bot traffic is small (2.0%–6.7% weekly on
  Cloudflare between May and September 2026; 1.7% on our measurement site
  over 36 days) and volatile.
- Automated traffic is behaviourally heterogeneous along measurable axes.
- Referral traffic from search to publishers declined measurably during
  2025–2026, with one causal estimate at −39.8% organic outbound clicks.
- Access-control and authentication mechanisms are in active development,
  with adoption ranging from near-universal (robots.txt) to near-zero
  consumption (llms.txt).

### 5.8.2 Forecasts, attributed

| Forecast | Source | Date | Horizon | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bots would exceed humans by end 2027, revised to early 2027; actual crossing ≈ April 2026 | Matthew Prince, Cloudflare CEO | 3 June 2026 | — | Operator extrapolation; the forecast was wrong by ~18 months in its own author's assessment |
| 40% of enterprise applications integrated with task-specific AI agents by end of 2026, from under 5% | Gartner (Anushree Verma) | 26 August 2025 | End 2026 | Analyst forecast |
| Agentic AI to drive ~30% of enterprise application software revenue by 2035, exceeding $450bn, from 2% in 2025 | Gartner | 26 August 2025 | 2035 | Analyst scenario model |

**No peer-reviewed model was found that projects the point at which
agentic traffic overtakes training traffic or human traffic.** Every
claim of that kind located during this work is an extrapolation published
by a vendor or an analyst.

The Prince forecast is instructive precisely because it failed: the same
operator with the best available data predicted the crossing point
eighteen months late. This is a reason for caution about all projections
in this area, including any this work might be tempted to make.

### 5.8.3 What cannot be established

The available sources permit the observation that a transformation is
under way. They do not permit a determination of what composition web
traffic will assume in the coming years, nor which of the access
mechanisms under development will prevail, nor whether the current
direction of infrastructure policy — identification, classification,
blocking, monetisation — will prove stable or be superseded.

## 5.9 Plausible Scenarios for Web Infrastructure

The following three scenarios are constructed as qualitative devices for
reasoning about design, not as predictions. No probability is assigned
and no preference is expressed.

### Scenario A — Incremental evolution

HTTP fetching of HTML remains the dominant access mode. Agents adapt to
existing mechanisms: they respect `robots.txt`, adopt authentication
where required, and negotiate content representations where available.
Infrastructure changes are refinements of what exists — better eviction
policies, better bot classification, conventional rate limiting.

*Design implication:* per-class handling remains useful but optional;
existing tooling suffices with parameter changes.

### Scenario B — Mediated web

A growing share of agent access occurs through intermediaries: search
indexes, tool APIs, MCP servers, syndication, or cached representations
held by the assistant provider. The agent frequently answers without
contacting the origin at all — the pattern observed, in a small sample,
in Section 5.5.

A concrete architectural proposal in this direction appeared while this
chapter was being written. Hua and Xiao's *Semantics Delivery Network*
(HotNets 2026) argues that semantic chunk retrieval should become a
first-class network-delivery abstraction, and reports that a page-first
retrieval path processes between two and three orders of magnitude more
content than the agent ultimately consumes. It is cited as evidence that
this scenario is being actively designed for, not as evidence that it will
obtain.

*Design implication:* origin request volume ceases to be a proxy for
interaction volume. Sites become invisible to their own audience
measurement, and the relationship between infrastructure cost and
delivered value is mediated by parties the site does not control.
Discoverability in an index matters more than reachability.

### Scenario C — Differentiated web

Publishers and infrastructure providers explicitly distinguish human,
search, training, and agent traffic, and apply different policy, pricing,
caching, and admission to each. This is the direction in which the
mechanisms of Section 5.6 and the vendor defaults of Section 5.7 currently
point.

*Design implication:* the correctness of per-class policy becomes
load-bearing. Classification errors and misestimated per-class costs
translate directly into misallocated capacity, and identity — which is
declarable and, as Section 5.6.4 notes, potentially forgeable — becomes
a control input.

### The common implication

The three scenarios differ in what infrastructure will need to do, but
they agree on one point: **in none of them is it safe to treat automated
traffic as a single homogeneous class.** Scenario A requires
distinguishing classes to tune policy; Scenario B requires understanding
which interactions are invisible; Scenario C makes class distinction the
basis of the entire control plane. The uncertainty about which scenario
obtains is therefore an argument for studying class heterogeneity, not
against it.

## 5.10 Implications for This Work

The external reconnaissance in this chapter does not itself constitute a
contribution. Its function is to establish that the questions posed by
the experimental part of this work are the right questions to ask about a
real system.

Five connections follow.

**A single "AI traffic" measure is insufficient.** Section 5.3 documents
that traffic aggregated under that label differs along locality, volume,
connection behaviour, and urgency. Any policy or capacity model
parameterised by a single AI-traffic fraction is parameterised on a
composite whose components behave differently — and, as Section 5.7.3
shows, whose composition can change materially within weeks.

**Behavioural differences may imply cost differences.** If classes access
content with different locality, they interact differently with a shared
cache, and therefore potentially impose different costs on the tiers
behind it. Whether they do, and by how much, is an empirical question
that external data cannot answer — it requires a controlled origin.
*The experimental chapters answer it, and the answer is less simple than
the question: the per-request cost of the agentic class is not a fixed
quantity at all, but changes sign as a function of the size of that class's
working set. The quantity measured in §5.3.5 is therefore not a descriptive
statistic but the input on which that cost depends.*

**Shared infrastructure makes class interaction relevant.** The classes
described in Section 5.3 do not arrive at separate systems. They contend
for the same cache and the same backend. Section 5.3.3 records that the
five largest operators on the measurement site requested essentially
identical sets of pages, which establishes that the precondition for
inter-class interaction — overlapping working sets — holds in practice
and is not an artefact of synthetic workload construction.

**If identity becomes a control input, its forgeability matters.**
Section 5.6.4 documents that a single source infrastructure was observed
presenting multiple declared AI operator identities, and that only one of
twenty-nine operators authenticated cryptographically. A control plane
that admits or refuses on the basis of a declared user-agent string is
building on an input that is, in the general case, unverified.

**If some agents do not fetch, request volume understates interaction.**
Section 5.5 documents, in a small exploratory test, that an assistant may
answer a question about a site without requesting the site. Request
volume measured at the origin is therefore a lower bound on interaction,
and the relationship between the two is not constant across operators.

These five points motivate the experimental questions addressed in the
following chapter. They do not answer them.

## 5.11 Conclusions

Five conclusions, stated at the level of confidence the sources support.

**Traffic on the Cloudflare network is already predominantly automated**,
measured as a share of HTML page requests, with corroborating evidence
from two other commercially independent measurement platforms using
different denominators on their own networks. The magnitude is uncertain
and the figure does not extend to the web as a whole; the direction is
not in dispute.

**"AI traffic" is not a homogeneous class.** Training, search, agentic,
SEO, and undeclared automation differ measurably in volume, coverage,
selectivity, connection behaviour, error profile, and arrival pattern.
The agentic class — the one in which a person is waiting — is the
smallest by every available measurement and the most often refused, and
its composition is itself unstable over periods of weeks.

**Infrastructure is moving toward identification, classification,
differentiated policy, and monetisation.** This direction is documented
in IETF working-group activity, in vendor defaults that took effect
during the writing of this chapter, and in publisher-side licensing
initiatives. The mechanisms are at very different stages of maturity, and
the gap between publication and consumption — most starkly visible in the
llms.txt data — suggests that declared mechanisms should not be assumed
to be operative ones.

**Future composition cannot be determined from available sources.** No
peer-reviewed projection exists; the best-informed operator forecast in
the field was wrong by eighteen months, as acknowledged by its author.
Three qualitatively different infrastructural futures remain consistent
with the evidence.

**This heterogeneity and this uncertainty together motivate studying the
cost and interaction of distinct traffic classes in shared
infrastructure.** If automated traffic were homogeneous, a single
aggregate policy would suffice. If its future composition were known, one
could design for that composition. Neither condition holds.
