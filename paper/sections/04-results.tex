\subsection{The marginal cost of one class changes sign with its reachable set}\label{sec:r-sign}
% Finding \claim{A1}: -0.0353 +/- 0.0053 -> +0.1605 +/- 0.0067 -> +0.4383 +/- 0.0055
%   origin requests per added agentic request, same class, volumes, cache, corpus.
% Replication (claims v3.6): the scope-0.02 point only, -0.0405 +/- 0.0061, t = -6.60.
% Caveat in the text, not only in the caption: this is the 12->36 req/s marginal; the net
%   effect 0->36 is NOT NEGATIVE (never "positive"): two measurements reported separately,
%   +0.392 +/- 0.158 [+0.0215, +0.7625] and +0.238 +/- 0.110 [-0.0601, +0.5361]
%   (\claim{A9}) — "reduces origin work" is retracted (R2).
% Honeypot placement by windowed working set (C3), details in Appendix.
In our testbed, under the separated mapping and the three scopes, the marginal origin
cost of the agentic class changes sign as its reachable set grows.\claim{A1}
When the agentic class is raised from 12 to 36 req/s, each added request costs
$-0.0353 \pm 0.0053$ origin requests with a reachable set of 0.19$\times$ the cache,
$+0.1605 \pm 0.0067$ at 0.58$\times$, and $+0.4383 \pm 0.0055$ at 1.93$\times$
(\cref{fig:sign}).\claim{A1}
Class, volumes, cache and corpus are identical across the three configurations; only
the reachable set changes.\claim{A1}

The reachable set of a class is the set of objects from which our load generator can draw
its requests (\cref{sec:m-classes}); it is neither the set of objects requested within a
given window nor the bytes the class occupies in the cache.
We set the agentic scope to 0.02, 0.06 and 0.20 of the corpus, that is 1,017, 3,051 and
10,170 objects, against a measured cache capacity of 5,263 objects.\claim{A1}\claim{C2}
At each scope the agentic rate goes from 12 to 36 req/s, the human and exhaustive classes
stay at 55 and 28 req/s, reachable sets are mapped separately across classes, and each point
is the mean of 5 runs.\claim{A1}
The marginal cost is the change in origin req/s divided by the change in the configured
offered rate of the class (\cref{sec:m-marginal}).

Consecutive points are well separated (Welch $t = 22.99$ and $t = 32.13$).\claim{A1}
The negative value at the smallest scope is distinguishable from zero
($t = -6.7$).\claim{A1}
We measured this point again, with all runs in one night, under a pre-registered protocol
(\cref{sec:m-prereg}).\claim{A1}
The replication gives $-0.0405 \pm 0.0061$ ($t = -6.60$), within three combined standard
errors of the original value.\claim{A1}
Only this point was replicated; the scopes 0.06 and 0.20 have one run per
point.\claim{A1}

This is a marginal cost between 12 and 36 req/s, not the net effect of the class.
At the smallest scope, the net effect of adding the agentic class from 0 to 36 req/s is
not negative.\claim{A9}
We measured it twice and report the two measurements separately.\claim{A9}
From runs on two different days it is $+0.392 \pm 0.158$ origin req/s (95\% CI $+0.02$ to
$+0.76$).\claim{A9}
In the same-day replication it is $+0.238 \pm 0.110$ origin req/s (95\% CI $-0.06$ to
$+0.54$), an interval that contains zero.\claim{A9}
A negative marginal cost therefore does not mean that agentic traffic lowers total origin
work in this configuration: adding it is not a saving.\claim{A9}

By windowed working set, the observed quantity rather than the reachable set, the smallest
scope is closest to the agentic clients of our public honeypot: at 12 agentic req/s it
touches about 693 distinct objects in a 143 s window, against a 90th percentile of 670 for honeypot agentic clients
(Appendix, \cref{fig:a-honeypot}).\claim{C3}
It is not close on contiguity.\claim{C5}
Measured the same way on both sides, as the share of consecutive requests within one
session that read adjacent chapters, 4.25\% of the request pairs of honeypot agentic
clients are contiguous, against 66.9\% for our generator; a session is a TCP connection
on the honeypot and a k6 virtual user in the generator
(\cref{sec:limitations}).\claim{C5}

\Cref{sec:r-accounting} asks where this change of sign comes from, term by term.\claim{B2}
\undertowfig{FIG-01_marginal-cost-vs-working-set}{Marginal origin cost versus reachable set}{fig:sign}

\subsection{Where the change comes from}\label{sec:r-accounting}
% \claim{B2}: in 12->36 the largest term is the agentic requests already present (-1.622);
%   sum and measurement differ by 1.6% (0->12) and about 5% (12->36), from
%   fig05_accounting.csv. Arithmetic, not mechanism.
To see which requests carry the change of sign, we split the measured change in origin
work at the smallest scope into four terms (\cref{fig:accounting}).\claim{B2}
The terms are the new agentic requests, the agentic requests already present, the human
class and the exhaustive class.\claim{B2}
Each term is the part of the change in origin req/s that falls on that group of
requests.\claim{B2}
We do this for two steps of agentic load, from 0 to 12 and from 12 to 36 req/s, with the
human and exhaustive classes fixed at 55 and 28 req/s.\claim{B2}\claim{A1}
The 0 req/s point comes from a run on a different day.\claim{A9}

In the step from 12 to 36 req/s, the largest term is the change on the 12 agentic req/s
already present: $-1.622$ origin req/s.\claim{B2}
The four terms sum to $-0.889$ origin req/s, against a measured change of
$-0.846$.\claim{B2}
The sum and the measurement differ by about 5\% in this step, and by 1.6\% in the step
from 0 to 12 req/s.\claim{B2}
This measured change is the same one that gives the marginal cost at the smallest scope
in \cref{sec:r-sign}, so the decomposition and the marginal agree.\claim{B2}\claim{A1}

The decomposition is accounting.
It states which terms make up the measured change and how large each one is.\claim{B2}
It does not state why a term takes the value it has.
The step from 0 to 12 req/s is shown next to the step from 12 to 36 req/s so that the
second is not read alone: from 0 to 36 req/s, the net change in origin work is not
negative (\cref{sec:r-sign}).\claim{A9}
\undertowfig{FIG-05_accounting-decomposition}{Accounting decomposition of the change in origin work}{fig:accounting}

\subsection{Raising one class moves that class, not the others}\label{sec:r-asymmetry}
% \claim{A2}: agentic 4.05x, exhaustive 1.03x, human 1.02x (13% -> 30% agentic share) at
%   scope 0.02; agentic 1.62x and 1.16x at 0.06 and 0.20 (tools/class_miss_by_scope.py).
%   Say explicitly that only the agentic volume moved (UNDERTOW-CONTESTO §4.2).
% \claim{A7}: exhaustive own-volume marginal flat (~0.98: 0.961 +/- 0.009, 0.997 +/- 0.010,
%   0.979 +/- 0.004, divisor = configured alpha*lambda, claims v3),
%   miss ratio 1.09x between 14 and 42 req/s (no exhaustive traffic at 0) — secondary
%   series: shared mapping, 1,211 s window. Closes the
%   "you varied only one class" objection.
% Position against Zhang et al.: they vary composition and measure hit ratio; we measure
%   marginal origin cost and compare three classes.
When the offered rate of the agentic class increases while the others stay fixed, the
per-request cost of the agentic class changes strongly and that of the other two classes
barely changes.\claim{A2}
Here the cost per request of a class is its miss ratio, the share of its requests that
reaches the origin (\cref{sec:m-marginal}).\claim{A2}
The runs are those of \cref{sec:r-sign}: only the agentic volume moves, from 12 to
36 req/s, while the human and exhaustive volumes stay fixed.\claim{A2}\claim{A1}

At the smallest scope, the agentic miss ratio falls from 0.1796 to 0.0444, a factor of
4.05 computed from the unrounded means (\cref{fig:asymmetry}a).\claim{A2}
Over the same step, the exhaustive miss ratio falls by a factor of 1.03 and the human
miss ratio rises by a factor of 1.02.\claim{A2}
The agentic factor depends on the scope: at 0.06 and 0.20 it drops to 1.62 and
1.16.\claim{A2}
At those scopes the human and exhaustive factors stay between 1.02 and 1.06.\claim{A2}

This design varies the volume of one class only.
A class whose own volume is never changed could look insensitive for that reason alone.
We therefore also vary the exhaustive volume on its own, from 0 to 42 req/s, with the
human and agentic volumes fixed (\cref{fig:asymmetry}b).\claim{A7}
The marginal cost of the exhaustive class is flat at about 0.98 origin requests per added
request: $0.961 \pm 0.009$, $0.997 \pm 0.010$ and $0.979 \pm 0.004$ over the three
steps.\claim{A7}
Between 14 and 42 req/s its miss ratio rises from 0.791 to 0.861, a factor of
1.09; at 0 req/s there is no exhaustive traffic and so no exhaustive miss
ratio.\claim{A7}

This is a secondary series.
It comes from a campaign of 16 September 2026 with shared mapping, a measurement window
of 1,211 s instead of 620 s, and 3 runs per point, so it is not paired with the primary
design.\claim{A7}
The agentic volume is fixed at 12 req/s at all four points, and the only manipulated
variable is the exhaustive volume.\claim{A7}
Varied on its own volume, the exhaustive class stays almost flat.\claim{A7}
The two series differ in mapping and window, so we compare the shape of their response,
not its size.\claim{A7}

Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025} already vary the share of AI traffic and measure the cache
hit ratio; what we add is the marginal origin cost and its comparison across three
coexisting classes.\claim{A2}
Because the other classes stay fixed, the total offered rate grows with the class we
raise; the design measures the response to one class's offered rate, not to its share at
constant total.\claim{A2}

\subsection{A confounder: overlap between reachable sets}\label{sec:r-overlap}
% \claim{A3}: +0.714 (t = 5.74) and +1.012 (t = 7.74) origin req/s when the agentic working
%   set is decorrelated from the human popular head (different days); same-day
%   replication +0.882 +/- 0.183 (t = 4.82) and +0.872 +/- 0.064 (t = 13.62). The
%   difference between the two rates does not recur: no claim that the effect grows with
%   the rate. Narrow wording: a bias in the experimental characterisation of a class's
%   cost (not "we discovered free-riding").
How cheap a class looks depends on how much its reachable set overlaps with the popular
head of another class.\claim{A3}
We measure the same configuration under two mappings of the agentic reachable set, with
the same class volumes.\claim{A3}
In the shared mapping, the agentic reachable set is drawn with the same permutation as the
human one, so it coincides with the human popular head.\claim{A3}
In the separated mapping, a different permutation removes most of this
overlap.\claim{A3}
In the original runs the two mappings were measured on different days: the shared runs
on 20 September 2026, before full configurations were recorded, and the separated runs on
21 September, as for the 0 req/s point of \cref{sec:r-sign}.\claim{A3}
The pre-registered replication measured both mappings in the same night
(\cref{sec:m-prereg}).\claim{A3}

Removing the overlap raises origin work at both agentic rates, in both measurements
(\cref{fig:overlap}).\claim{A3}
In the original runs the increase is $+0.714$ req/s at 12 agentic req/s ($t = 5.74$) and
$+1.012$ req/s at 36 agentic req/s ($t = 7.74$).\claim{A3}
In the same-day replication it is $+0.882 \pm 0.183$ req/s ($t = 4.82$) and
$+0.872 \pm 0.064$ req/s ($t = 13.62$).\claim{A3}
The difference between the two rates seen in the original runs does not recur, so we make
no claim that the effect grows with the agentic rate.\claim{A3}
Under the shared mapping, the agentic class at the same volume therefore appears cheaper
than under the separated one.\claim{A3}

We read this result narrowly.
The overlap is a bias in the experimental characterisation of a class's cost: it changes
how much origin work a measurement attributes to the class.\claim{A3}
It is not a property of the class, and we do not claim that it describes how agentic
clients behave on the Web.\claim{A3}
What it shows is that the cost measured for a class depends on a choice of the
experiment that is easy to leave implicit: whether the reachable sets of different classes
are drawn from the same popularity ranking.\claim{A3}
For this reason the primary design of this paper uses the separated
mapping.\claim{A1}
Outside this comparison, series measured under the shared mapping are reported as
secondary and labelled as such, as in \cref{sec:r-asymmetry}.\claim{A7}

% \claim{C4}: isolated run tre-20260911-154109 (agentic class alone, warm cache) against the
%   separated-mapping mixture of 21 Sep at 13% agentic share.
The other classes shape the measured cost of a class in a second way.\claim{C4}
Measured alone, with a warm cache, the agentic class has a miss ratio of 0.002.\claim{C4}
In the mixture of the primary design, at 13\% of the load, its miss ratio is
0.180.\claim{C4}
The cost of a class measured in isolation therefore does not predict its cost when it
shares the cache with other classes.\claim{C4}

\subsection{Consequence for admission policies}\label{sec:r-frontier}
% \claim{A6}: blocking and deferring lie on one convex frontier (slopes 0.01 -> 102.25 ms
%   per 1,000 served). \claim{A4}: B vs C, +2,807 +/- 185 served (t = 15.19), p99
%   +0.03 +/- 0.73 ms, CI [-1.99, +2.05]. Never "dominates". Why blocking one class frees
%   more origin work than the other (A5, SOSTENUTO) is in Mechanism, not here.
% The no-policy point (SE 48.6 ms) is not in claims.md: not stated in the text.
At the knee of the testbed, blocking and deferring are not alternative policies: they
lie on one empirical work/latency frontier sampled at one operating point, between
requests served and human tail latency.\claim{A6}
We measure eight admission policies at 185 req/s, with 3 runs each
(\cref{fig:frontier}).\claim{A6}\claim{A4}
The policies are no policy, two blocking policies, and five budgets for deferring
exhaustive requests.\claim{A6}
Ordered by requests served, the marginal slope of the frontier rises monotonically, from
0.01 to 102.25 ms of human p99 per 1,000 additional requests served.\claim{A6}
No point lies off the curve.\claim{A6}
At this operating point, blocking and deferring are therefore two parameterisations of
the same trade-off.\claim{A6}

The two blocking policies differ in requests served, not in measured latency.
Policy B blocks the exhaustive class; policy C blocks the agentic class as
well.\claim{A4}
B serves $2{,}807 \pm 185$ more requests than C ($t = 15.19$).\claim{A4}
The difference in human p99 between the two is $+0.03 \pm 0.73$ ms.\claim{A4}
Its 95\% confidence interval, $-1.99$ to $+2.05$ ms, contains zero.\claim{A4}
Blocking the agentic class in addition to the exhaustive class thus costs served
requests and gives no difference in human p99 that we can
measure.\claim{A4}
With 3 runs per policy, a latency difference of about 2 ms in either direction cannot be
excluded.\claim{A4}

These results say what changes at the origin and by how much.
\Cref{sec:mechanism} asks why.
