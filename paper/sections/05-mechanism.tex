\Cref{sec:results} states what changes at the origin; this section asks why.
Each statement carries its status.
A \emph{supported} statement is a strict inference from the measurements, with a
mechanism; an \emph{interpretive} one is consistent with the data but was not verified as
a prediction.
We also report the prediction that failed and the part of the mechanism that we did not
measure.

\subsection{From access pattern to marginal cost}\label{sec:m-chain}
% B1 is SOSTENUTO in claims.md; here only the two measured relations are stated as
% supported, and the rest of the chain as interpretive (weaker than the row).
The label alone does not determine marginal cost in this testbed.\claim{B1}
Two relations are measured, and we state them as supported.\claim{B1}
First, with the same label, volumes, cache and corpus, the agentic marginal changes sign as
only the reachable set grows (\cref{sec:r-sign}).\claim{A1}
Second, the per-request cost of a class responds to its own offered rate in a way that
differs across classes: the sensitivity of the agentic miss ratio falls as the reachable
set grows (\cref{sec:r-asymmetry}), while the exhaustive class, which never returns to a
chapter before it has traversed the corpus, has a flat marginal of about 0.98 when its own
offered rate is varied.\claim{A2}\claim{A7}
The rest is interpretive: we read the two relations as parts of one chain, in which the
access pattern of a class sets the geometry of its reachable set, the geometry sets how the
class interacts with the state of the shared cache, and that interaction sets the marginal
cost.\claim{B1}
We did not measure the intermediate links separately.
The chain would be falsified by a class with the same offered rate and the same reachable
set that behaves differently.\claim{B1}

The accounting of \cref{sec:r-accounting} shows where the change of sign sits.
In the step from 12 to 36 agentic req/s at the smallest scope, the largest term is the
change on the 12 agentic req/s already present: $-1.658$ of a measured total of $-0.834$
origin req/s.\claim{B2}
By construction this term is the offered rate already present times the change in the
agentic miss ratio, which falls from 0.1830 to 0.0448 over the same step.\claim{B2}\claim{A2}
The accounting states which term carries the change, not why that term takes its
value.

\subsection{The characteristic-time approximation}\label{sec:m-tc}
The characteristic-time approximation of LRU caches, introduced by
Fagin~\cite{fagin1977}, rediscovered and named by Che et al.~\cite{che2002} and
formalised by Fricker et al.~\cite{fricker2012}, treats an object as a hit if it is
requested again within a fixed time $T_C$ of its previous request.
Before the first results at scopes 0.06 and 0.20 were available, we used it to predict
the elasticity of the agentic miss ratio, that is, its miss ratio at 12 req/s over that
at 36 req/s.\claim{B3}
The prediction has no independent timestamp (\cref{sec:m-prereg}); we call it a prior
prediction.\claim{B3}
It gave 3.1 at scope 0.06 and 1.4 at scope 0.20, with a tolerance of $\pm$30\%
(derivation in \cref{app:tc}).\claim{B3}
We observe 1.66 and 1.15 (\cref{fig:a-model}).\claim{B3}
At scope 0.06 the observed value is 47\% below the prediction, outside the tolerance, so
the quantitative prediction is rejected; the earlier sweep, with the shared traversal
counter, gave the same verdict (1.62, 48\% below).\claim{B3}

Interpretive: the same approximation explains the ordering qualitatively.\claim{B4}
The wider the reachable set, the smaller the elasticity: 4.08 at scope 0.02, 1.66 at 0.06
and 1.15 at 0.20, and below 2.0 at the widest scope.\claim{B4}
In the terms of the approximation, a narrow reachable set lets more of a class's own
requests return within $T_C$ as its volume grows, and a wide one less so.\claim{B4}
This reading is consistent with the ordering but was not verified as a prediction; a
configuration with non-monotone elasticities would contradict it.\claim{B4}

% claims.md B7 (INTERPRETATIVO, post hoc): docs/RISULTATO-che-posthoc.md (51b2717),
% tools/che_posthoc.py. No parameter fitted to the lab (checked in the script, 30 Sep).
Interpretive, and computed after the measurements: the same approximation shows why a
negative marginal is not an anomaly.\claim{B7}
An object requested at rate $\lambda$ misses with probability $e^{-\lambda T}$, so it
produces misses at rate $\lambda e^{-\lambda T}$, which rises and then falls as $\lambda$
grows, with its maximum at $\lambda T = 1$; a class that asks often for few objects can
lower the misses of its own objects as its rate grows.
This is a property of the classical approximation, not a result of ours.
We fed it the access distribution of our generator and the three classes, with $T$ solved
from the capacity of the cache in bytes and no fitted parameters (\cref{app:tc}).\claim{B7}
Both versions of the exhaustive class that we computed reproduce the change of sign, and
the periodic one, which matches our corrected generator, reproduces the measured values
within 0.008 (\cref{tab:tc}).\claim{B7}
At scope 0.02 the mean $\lambda T$ of an agentic object moves from 1.23 at 12 req/s to
3.49 at 36, across the maximum, while at scope 0.20 it stays well below 1.\claim{B7}
The change of sign is therefore structural rather than an artefact of our testbed.\claim{B7}
The calculation was made after the data; it does not revive the rejected prior prediction,
which used a single $T$ and a uniform access rate.\claim{B3}\claim{B7}

\subsection{How the cost reaches the human class}\label{sec:m-human}
Supported: when the agentic reachable set widens, the human class pays through origin load,
not through lost cache hits.\claim{B5}
At 30\% agentic share, with volumes and cache fixed, the human p99 latency rises from
72.9 to 99.6\,ms as the scope grows from 0.02 to 0.20 ($+26.7 \pm 1.3$\,ms,
$t = 20.3$; \cref{fig:a-human}).\claim{B5}
Over the same range the human miss ratio changes only by a factor of 1.07, while the
origin rate rises from 37.4 to 52.4 req/s.\claim{B5}
A human hit ratio that fell in proportion to the p99 would contradict this
reading.\claim{B5}

Not supported: the channel through which origin load reaches human latency.\claim{B6}
It could be Varnish, its connections to the application, or the application's thread
pool.\claim{B6}
We infer the effect of load by exclusion and never measured the queue at each stage, so
we do not claim a channel (\cref{sec:limitations}).\claim{B6}

\subsection{Why blocking by class works for one class}\label{sec:m-block}
Supported: at the operating point of the empirical frontier, blocking one exhaustive request frees
about 0.82 origin requests, and blocking one agentic request about 0.10.\claim{A5}
The inference combines the miss ratios measured at that point, the agentic marginal cost,
and the flat marginal of about 0.98 that the exhaustive class shows when its own volume is
varied.\claim{A5}\claim{A7}
It explains the two blocking policies of \cref{sec:r-frontier}.
Blocking the exhaustive class by its identity removes about 0.82 origin requests per
blocked request, and that policy lies on the frontier.\claim{A5}\claim{D2}
Blocking the agentic class as well removes about 0.10 per blocked request: it costs $2{,}807 \pm 185$ served requests and gives no difference in human p99 that we can
measure.\claim{A4}\claim{A5}

\subsection{A simulator for what the testbed cannot vary}\label{sec:m-sim}
% Status SIMULATO (claims.md, rows S1-S6). Never evidence for the measured results.
% Details and the full matrix of the second phase: appendix app:sim.
To study configurations that the testbed cannot vary, we built a trace-driven simulator:
a generator that reproduces the one of \cref{sec:m-classes} and an LRU cache with the
byte capacity of the testbed's 128\,MiB.\claim{S1}
A first, pre-registered phase checked it against the testbed: within tolerances fixed in
advance, it reproduces the per-class miss ratios, the origin rate, and the sign and size
of the agentic marginal and of the overlap effect (\cref{app:sim}).\claim{S1}
With the traversal corrected, its predictions for the reruns of 28 and 29 September fell
within their intervals (\cref{sec:m-prereg}).\claim{A1}\claim{A9}

A second, pre-registered phase asked whether these dependences are specific to LRU and to
our synthetic sessions, crossing four eviction policies, LRU, SIEVE, S3-FIFO and W-TinyLFU,
with two session models, the generator's and sessions calibrated on the
honeypot.\claim{S2}
In the simulator, the dependence of the agentic marginal on the reachable set holds in 7 of
the 8 combinations, the exception being W-TinyLFU with the generator's
sessions.\claim{S2}
The negative sign at scope 0.02 holds with LRU, SIEVE and S3-FIFO and is indeterminate with
W-TinyLFU; our prior prediction that it is a property of LRU under scans is
refuted.\claim{S3}
The overlap effect is positive at 12 agentic req/s in all 8 combinations, and at 36 req/s it
holds only with LRU.\claim{S4}
The net effect of adding the agentic class is positive with LRU and W-TinyLFU and negative
with SIEVE and S3-FIFO, where it comes from fewer misses of the other classes; this is not a
demonstrated saving and says nothing about the Web.\claim{S5}
The human miss ratio grows with the agentic reachable set and rate under every policy
except S3-FIFO at the smallest scope, where it falls.\claim{S6}
In the simulator, then, what one more request of a class costs depends both on the state of
the cache and on its eviction policy (\cref{tab:sim}).\claim{S2}\claim{S5}
These are simulated results for policies that were not validated on the testbed, and they
are not evidence for the measured results of \cref{sec:results}.\claim{S2}
