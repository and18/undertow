\subsection{Testbed}\label{sec:m-testbed}
The testbed is a chain of four components on one virtual machine with 6 physical ARM
cores (Ampere Neoverse-N1, no SMT): a k6 load generator, a Varnish~7.5
cache, a gunicorn/Flask application and a PostgreSQL~16 database, each in its own
container.
Every component is pinned to fixed cores: the generator and the metrics collectors to
core~0, Varnish to core~1, the application to core~2 and PostgreSQL to cores
3--5.\claim{C1}
The application runs one gunicorn process with 8 threads and a pool of 2 to 12 database
connections.
Varnish keeps objects in 128\,MiB of memory and evicts them in LRU order.
Its configuration is deliberately minimal: no normalisation of the cache key, no grace or
keep period, and cacheability set only by the \texttt{Cache-Control} header of the
application.

The corpus is 495 public-domain books from Project Gutenberg, split into 16,954 chapters
and stored in PostgreSQL.\claim{A1}
All experiments request the chapter page.
It is cacheable for 86,400\,s, longer than any run, and a miss costs the application four
queries: the chapter, the book's metadata, its table of contents, and excerpts of the
linked chapters with a full-text headline.
Every class requests this same page and triggers the same four queries on a miss, so what
a miss costs depends on the size of the chapter, not on the class that requests it.
% Mean chapter size: data/derived/chapter_sizes.csv (response bodies reconstructed by
% tools/sim/object_sizes.py), separated mapping, distinct chapters of each reachable set;
% computed 28 Sep, decided 29 Sep. Not a claims.md row.
The reconstructed response of a chapter has a mean of 23.3, 24.4 and 25.5\,kB in the
agentic reachable sets at scopes 0.02, 0.06 and 0.20 (\cref{sec:m-classes}), against
25.5\,kB over the whole corpus, with medians close to 20.2\,kB in all four; the smallest
reachable set therefore holds chapters about 8\% smaller on average than the corpus.
The cache holds $5{,}263 \pm 8$ objects, with a mean of 24.9\,KiB of storage per object:
this is the mean number of objects in Varnish over the ten measurement windows in which
the cache is full.\claim{C2}
We measure it rather than estimate it from the configured size.\claim{C2}

On this testbed, origin requests are a proxy for backend load.
Across five admission policies that span a 4.5$\times$ range of load, the CPU used by
PostgreSQL, in cores, follows $0.021 + 0.0368 \times$ origin req/s ($R^2 = 0.997$,
largest residual 3.1\%).\claim{C1}
We claim this relation for this testbed only, not for backend load in
general.\claim{C1}

\subsection{Three traffic classes}\label{sec:m-classes}
k6 generates load in open loop at a constant arrival rate $\lambda$: a request is sent on
schedule whether or not earlier requests have completed.
Each request is assigned to a class by one uniform draw: exhaustive with probability
$\alpha$, agentic with probability $\beta$ and human otherwise.
The configured offered rates of the three classes are therefore $\alpha\lambda$,
$\beta\lambda$ and $(1-\alpha-\beta)\lambda$.
Each class sends its own \texttt{User-Agent}, and the generator records, per class,
whether each request was a hit or a miss from a diagnostic header that Varnish adds to
the response.
The classes differ only in which chapters they request.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Human.} A popularity rank $r = \lfloor N^{u} \rfloor$ is drawn with $u$
  uniform on $[0,1]$ and $N = 16{,}954$: a log-uniform distribution, approximately
  Zipf(1), that never draws rank~0.\claim{A3}
  The rank is mapped to a chapter by the permutation $(r \cdot m_H + s) \bmod N$, with
  $m_H = 2654435761$ and seed $s$.
\item \emph{Exhaustive.} The class traverses the corpus in a fixed pseudo-random order,
  one new chapter per request, so no chapter is requested twice before the whole corpus
  has been visited.
  The measurement phase continues the traversal where the warm-up left it.
  Since 28 September 2026 the class runs as its own k6 scenario, with its own traversal
  counter (see below).
\item \emph{Agentic.} The class requests short sessions of three consecutive chapters in
  corpus order.
  Each k6 virtual user keeps its own session.
  A session starts from a rank drawn with a power law close to Zipf(0.6) among the first
  $\lfloor \mathrm{scope} \cdot N \rfloor$ ranks, mapped to a chapter by
  $(r \cdot m_A + s) \bmod N$.
  We call the chapters that the generator can request for the class its \emph{reachable
  set}: $3\lfloor \mathrm{scope} \cdot N \rfloor$ chapters, that is 1,017, 3,051 and
  10,170 at scopes 0.02, 0.06 and 0.20, or 0.19$\times$, 0.58$\times$ and 1.93$\times$
  the cache capacity.\claim{A1}\claim{C2}
  % CHECK with the A1 update: at scope 0.20 the 10,170 positions contain 10,020 distinct
  % chapters (sessions of adjacent bases overlap), i.e. 1.90x; at 0.02 and 0.06 the counts
  % are exact. claims.md A1 says 10,170 and 1.93x: not changed here.
\end{itemize}
We keep three quantities apart.
The \emph{reachable set} is the variable we manipulate: it is fixed by the generator's
configuration, whether or not every object in it is requested during a run.
The \emph{windowed working set} is observed: the distinct objects that a client or a class
actually requests within a time window, which is what we can measure on the honeypot
(\cref{sec:m-honeypot}).\claim{C3}
The \emph{occupancy} of the cache is the number of bytes that Varnish holds, bounded by its
128\,MiB.
We express the reachable set as a multiple of the capacity in objects, not in bytes,
because the capacity itself was measured as a number of objects and every class requests
the same kind of object, with a mean of 24.9\,KiB of storage per object.\claim{C2}
To change the volume of one class while the others stay fixed in absolute terms, we
change $\lambda$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ together.\claim{A1}

\paragraph{An artefact of the exhaustive traversal, and its correction.}
% docs/retractions.md R7; docs/PREREG-lab-trav-own-20260928.md (204459c);
% docs/RISULTATO-lab-trav-own-20260928.md; claims.md v3.7 (A3, A9, B2, D8).
In the runs before 28 September 2026, the exhaustive class took its position in the
traversal from an iteration counter shared by all three classes.
Its path through the corpus therefore depended on the total offered rate $\lambda$, which
changes between our configurations even when the exhaustive rate is fixed.\claim{A9}\claim{D8}
Our simulator of the generator exposed the effect, and the rerun described below measured
it: the drop in the exhaustive miss ratio as agentic traffic grows remains, at about 57\% of
its previous size.\claim{D8}
We corrected the generator by running the exhaustive class as a separate k6 scenario with
its own counter, which also makes its arrivals regular instead of randomly
interleaved.
A pre-registered rerun of the five configurations at scope 0.02, on 28 September 2026,
confirmed the predicted shifts; at that scope these runs are primary, and the earlier runs
are kept as a previous measurement.\claim{A3}\claim{A9}\claim{D8}
The artefact moved the net effect of the agentic class and the size of the exhaustive
drop; it did not move the overlap effect beyond its errors.\claim{A9}\claim{D8}\claim{A3}
In the primary design the human and exhaustive classes are offered 55 and 28 req/s, and
the agentic class 12 or 36 req/s (configured values 11.9985 and 35.9975).\claim{A1}

\paragraph{Shared and separated mapping.}
The multiplier $m_A$ decides where the agentic reachable set lies with respect to human
popularity.
With $m_A = m_H$, the \emph{shared} mapping, agentic sessions start on the most popular
human chapters.
With $m_A = 3266489917$, the \emph{separated} mapping, they start in a region of the
corpus uncorrelated with the human ranks.\claim{A3}
Computed from the generator's distribution at scope 0.02, the overlap between agentic
session starts and the human popular head is 99.7\% under the shared mapping and 2.1\%
under the separated one; the share of human traffic that falls on agentic session starts
is 59.8\% and 1.0\%.\claim{A3}
The primary design uses the separated mapping.\claim{A1}
The two mappings meet only in the comparison between them (\cref{sec:r-overlap}); no
other comparison mixes a shared and a separated measurement.\claim{A3}
The shared-mapping runs of 20 September 2026 were launched as separated runs, but the
multiplier was not passed to the generator, which used the default $m_A = m_H$.
We use them only as shared-mapping runs.\claim{A3}

\subsection{Marginal cost}\label{sec:m-marginal}
The origin rate of a run is the number of requests that miss in Varnish and reach the
application, summed over the three classes and divided by the length of the measurement
window.
The miss ratio of a class is the share of its successful requests that miss.
Every marginal cost in this paper is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marginal}
  m \;=\; \frac{\Delta\,(\text{origin req/s})}{\Delta\,(\text{configured offered rate of the class})},
\end{equation}
between two configurations that differ only in the offered rate of that class.
The divisor is the configured rate, $\beta\lambda$ or $\alpha\lambda$, read from the
configuration of the run, never the rounded nominal step: for the agentic class of the
primary design it is $35.9975 - 11.9985 = 23.9990$ req/s.\claim{A1}
For the exhaustive class the configured rates are 0, 13.9968, 27.9965 and
41.9977 req/s.\claim{A7}
A marginal cost is measured between two non-zero rates and is not the net effect of the
class; we report the net effect separately (\cref{sec:r-sign}).\claim{A9}

\subsection{Measurement protocol}\label{sec:m-protocol}
A run is one configuration measured several times; each measurement is a repetition.
Each repetition starts from an empty cache, because Varnish is restarted.
It then runs 300\,s of warm-up, whose results are discarded, and a measurement window of
620\,s.
The code lengthens the window when the exhaustive class needs longer to traverse the
whole corpus once; this never happens in the primary design.
The secondary series that varies the exhaustive volume uses a window of
1,211\,s.\claim{A7}
Primary runs and the replication have 5 repetitions; the admission-policy frontier and
the exhaustive series have 3.\claim{A1}\claim{A4}\claim{A7}
The origin rate does not depend on the length of the window: 39.84, 40.03 and
40.28 req/s over windows of 620, 1,860 and 3,720\,s.\claim{C7}

A repetition passes the validity gate if k6 dropped no scheduled request and fewer than
1\% of requests failed.

Since 21 September 2026 each run stores its full configuration and the code version.
The configurations of earlier runs are not recorded: where we use them, the rates are
reconstructed from the per-class request counts in the k6 output, and we say
so.\claim{A9}\claim{A3}
Some comparisons join runs from different days.
The replication (\cref{sec:m-prereg}) re-measured five such configurations: the origin rate
moved by at most 0.154 req/s, within 1.17 standard errors.\claim{A3}\claim{A9}
This supports the comparisons between those two sets of days only; drift within one night
is not measured.\claim{A3}\claim{A9}

\subsection{Statistics}\label{sec:m-stats}
Each point is the mean over its repetitions, with standard error $\mathrm{SE} = s/\sqrt{n}$.
The SE of a difference between two points is $\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_1^2 + \mathrm{SE}_2^2}$;
we test it with Welch's $t$, with degrees of freedom from the Welch--Satterthwaite
formula, and give 95\% confidence intervals with the matching Student quantile.
The SE of a marginal cost is the SE of the difference divided by the step in offered rate.
Ratios and elasticities are computed from unrounded means, never from rounded values.

\subsection{Honeypot}\label{sec:m-honeypot}
To place the synthetic agentic class against real clients, we run a honeypot on a public
measurement site, a static site of chapters of public-domain books.
Its web server logs, for each request, the time, URL, status and user agent, an
identifier of the TCP connection with the request's position in it, and the Web Bot Auth
headers \texttt{Signature-Agent} and \texttt{Signature-Input}.\claim{C6}
Clients are classed by their declared user agent into six classes; the \emph{agent}
class matches fetchers that act for a user, such as \texttt{ChatGPT-User},
\texttt{Claude-User} and Perplexity.
The declaration is not verified.
All aggregates come from one fingerprinted snapshot of the logs and a fixed window,
[12 August, 22 September 2026) UTC, with 520,871 requests.\claim{C6}
Client addresses are used only in memory, as a grouping key through a keyed hash with a
fresh random key; no address, hash or log line is written, and only per-class aggregates
leave the analysis.

We compare the honeypot with the generator on two quantities.
The windowed working set of a client is the largest number of distinct URLs it requests
within a sliding window of 143\,s, for the clients with at least 5 requests.\claim{C3}
The window is the characteristic time of the testbed cache, fixed from an earlier capacity
estimate of 5,274 objects; with 5,263 it would be 142\,s, a difference of 0.2\% that we
do not recompute.\claim{C3}
Contiguity is the share of consecutive request pairs within one session that read
adjacent chapters of the same book.\claim{C5}
A session is a TCP connection on the honeypot and a k6 virtual user in the generator.\claim{C5}

\subsection{Pre-registered replication}\label{sec:m-prereg}
The three results that rest on one run per point or on runs from different days (A1 at
scope 0.02, the overlap effect and the net effect) were measured again in one night, on
25--26 September 2026.\claim{A1}\claim{A3}\claim{A9}
The protocol was committed to the repository before launch, and its version is recorded
in the configuration of every run.\claim{A1}
It fixed the five configurations, identical to the original runs; their order, chosen so
that a linear drift during the night least affects the comparisons for A1 and the net
effect; the formulas; and the criteria.
